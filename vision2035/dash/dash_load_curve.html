What this means for generation planning

1 · The system is short today, not tomorrow. Over six months, 49.9 GWh (4.1% of demand) went unserved and load was shed in 27% of all hours, with shedding events up to 155 MW. True demand is therefore higher than billed energy, and any forecast built on sales data will understate it.

2 · Size against ~360 MW peak and a very flat curve. Robust peak ≈ 358 MW, average 282 MW, load factor 74%. Demand never drops below ~180 MW in normal operation. That minimum is a natural anchor for baseload or firm capacity (domestic thermal such as Kekeli, plus firm imports), with roughly 180 MW of swing between night valley and evening peak.

3 · The binding hour is 19h–22h. The system peak and two-thirds of unserved energy are in the evening. Solar (e.g. Blitta-type) displaces daytime thermal and import energy but does not close the deficit; each solar addition should be paired with 4h storage or dispatchable capacity to shift into the 18h–01h window.

4 · A first-cut supply stack to test. Firm capacity (domestic + contracted imports) covering ~300 MW would eliminate ~90% of current shedding hours; peaking or storage for the last ~60 MW above that runs under 10% of hours, which favors batteries or reciprocating engines over CCGT for that tranche. Demand growth then shifts the whole stack up: at 6–8%/yr, peak reaches ~450–490 MW by 2030 and ~640–730 MW by 2035.

5 · Data caveats before this goes external. The 383 MW spike (5 Jun 16h) and the 75 MW collapse (27 Jun 13h, likely a grid incident) should be verified with CEET before being quoted; deficit figures are integers and look logged rather than telemetered.
